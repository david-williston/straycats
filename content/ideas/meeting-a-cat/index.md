## The idea

When people meet a cat, most of them take a photo. That photo is the easiest way in there
will ever be, so Stray Cats should start there:

1. **See a cat, take one photo.** That's the whole first step. No form to fill in.
2. **Get the cat's card in seconds:** its photo, a name, its coat, and where it was met, at
   the level of the neighbourhood, never the exact spot.
3. **If the cat is already known, meet it properly.** "This might be Mountain Lion. Real name
   unknown. Others have met this cat here too." You learn its story instead of starting from
   nothing.
4. **If nobody has met it yet,** you're the first. You can give it a name, and your name for
   it joins the others; it doesn't replace them.

You can start with just a nickname: no email, no password. And every card starts from a real
photo of a real cat that someone actually met.

## Cats you've met

Everyone who uses Stray Cats would keep a list of the cats they've met. Not cats they own
or have claimed: cats they've *met*. It's a small, warm thing to look back on, and it's
the start of a relationship with each one.

## Sharing a cat

You could send a cat's card to a friend in a message, or to the next person who'll be
looking after the house. They can then **follow** that cat: see when it's been met again,
and its story as it grows. A card only ever shows what's safe to share (see
[Privacy & Animal Safety](/ideas/privacy-and-safety/)).

You could also see cats being met in other towns and countries: a gentle window on how many
people, everywhere, are quietly looking out for cats. Only the area is ever shown, and only
after a delay, so it can never lead anyone to a cat.

## Learning to see cats

Once you start meeting cats, you start *seeing* them: the tabby's M on the forehead, the
tuxedo's white chest, the calico's three colours, the notched ear. Stray Cats could help
people learn coats and markings a little at a time, and keep track of the ones they've met.

That isn't only for fun. Coats and markings are exactly what helps tell one cat from another
(see [Cat Identity & Multiple Names](/ideas/cat-identity/)). The more people notice, the
better everyone gets at recognising each cat.

## Gentle reasons to look

A few light prompts could make a walk a chance to notice the cats around you: "Have you seen
Ninja this week?", "Meet a cat on a street you don't usually walk." Cats met while travelling
could be kept too: the cat outside a café in another town is part of someone's memory, and
part of that cat's story.

## What it's for

The pleasure of meeting a cat is the doorway, not the destination. Every meeting is an
[encounter](/ideas/encounters/), and every encounter quietly helps the cat:

- **It adds to the cat's history,** so the next person inherits what's known.
- **It helps recognise the cat:** "Is this Mountain Lion?" is a moment of fun for one person
  and a confirmed match for the cat's record.
- **It can carry a quick note about the cat's wellbeing:** looks thin, limping, ear tipped
  (already sterilised), new kittens nearby. That's what the
  [care network](/ideas/care-network/) needs to know.
- **It shows who cares:** a cat met by many people has many people who might help.

## What it won't do

- **No popularity contests.** No rankings, no "most-met cat", nothing that turns a cat into
  an attraction. Attention can put a cat at risk.
- **No exact spots.** Cards show the neighbourhood, never the exact place where a cat lives
  or eats, and never landmarks that give it away.
- **No public photo links.** Photos are shown inside cards, never as open links anyone can
  pass around.
- **Never at the cat's expense.** If a fun feature and the cat's safety disagree, the cat
  wins.

## Open questions

- What's the least a card needs to show to be useful, and safe?
- How do you invite people to add something useful (a health note, "this cat is missing")
  without turning a nice moment into a chore?
- Should the first person to meet a cat be remembered on its card?
- How do you keep the fun from ever becoming the point?
