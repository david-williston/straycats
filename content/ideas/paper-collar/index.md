*An idea for a simple, cheap way to ask a friendly cat's owner to get in touch.*

I first saw the paper collar in a post by a moderator in the Facebook group Table View Pets
lost & found, in Cape Town, on 23 July 2025. I thought it was a new idea. It isn't.

## What a paper collar is

It is a strip of paper, worn loosely around the neck of a friendly cat who keeps turning up,
that asks one question: **"Is this cat yours?"** It also gives the finder's contact details. If
the cat has an owner who has been searching, the owner can see the strip and get in touch.

## Not a new idea

Plenty of organizations already publish free printable versions, including
[Cats Protection](https://www.cats.org.uk/media/sadhln2p/found-a-cat-printable-collar.pdf) and
[Wood Green](https://woodgreen.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/Stray-Collar.pdf) in the UK,
[International Cat Care](https://icatcare.org/articles/found-a-cat),
[Austin Animal Services](https://austintexas.gov/node/73180), and others, such as Ottawa
Humane Society, Regina Humane Society and Animal Register NZ. A
[thread on the Maddie's Fund forum](https://forum.maddiesfund.org/discussion/have-you-heard-of-the-paper-collar-method)
credits the Catch-All Cat blog and the Community Cats Podcast for spreading it.

I won't copy anyone's wording. The honest question is what StrayCats could add.

## What StrayCats could add

StrayCats connects people who care for cats. A paper collar is a way to reach someone who
isn't on the platform yet: an owner who has no idea their cat has been visiting a stranger, or
a neighbour who also feeds the same cat.

The strip could carry the StrayCats website and a line on how to join, next to a space for the
finder's contact details. Whoever finds the collar lands in the same place as the person who
put it on. (As in the [open identity idea](/ideas/open-cat-identity/), the finder side wouldn't
need an app.)

The picture at the top is a concept mockup, not a photo and not a real product. It shows the
idea: a sheet of strips, one torn out and on the cat.

Here is how it could grow, from cheapest to most ambitious:

1. **A free printable template.** Print it at home, cut it out, write on it. Not built yet.
2. **Copies for veterinary clinics, shelters and community cat groups** to hand out, ideally
   alongside a free microchip scan. This costs almost nothing.
3. **Ready-made sheets.** Pre-printed strips that tear off cleanly. *Perforated* means a line
   of small holes to tear along; *die-cut* means cut through in a custom shape; *scored* means
   creased. Ideally the material would shrug off rain but still tear easily. Tyvek wristbands
   are the closest ready-made product I found, but Tyvek is built to resist tearing, which is
   the opposite of what a breakaway collar needs, so I wouldn't use it. Thin paper, or a
   water-resistant stock that tears with a light pull, would need testing. Custom print runs
   usually need minimums of a few hundred, and I haven't confirmed prices.
4. **Packs by mail.** Perhaps drop-shipped by a printing company, in different designs, with
   plenty of replacements for cats that keep losing their collars. I don't know whether
   print-on-demand services offer perforated strips. That needs quotes, so treat it
   as "could one day", after the free template and the vet copies.

The closure matters too: a slot-and-tab, or a loop through a slit, so there is no tape on
fur. It should be deliberately weak, so it tears if the cat gets caught. In other words, a
breakaway design.

## Safety comes first

A paper collar is a small intervention on a living animal. These precautions seem sensible:

- **Only for friendly cats** who are comfortable being handled. Never a nervous or feral cat.
- **Thin paper, not card**, so it tears if snagged.
- **Don't cover the whole strip in tape.** Leave part untaped so it can tear. Some guides tape
  over the writing to protect it from rain; a water-resistant material seems safer to me.
- **Loose fit**: about two fingers should slide under it, and looser is safer.
- **No tape directly against fur.**
- **Not for kittens.** This is a reasonable precaution of mine, not something I've seen sourced.
- **Check on the cat**, and take the collar off if it bothers them.
- **How long to leave it on** varies a lot between guides, from hours to a week or two. I won't
  invent a number.

## If nobody responds

Ask a vet or shelter to scan the whole body for a microchip. Chips can migrate, and a chip may
be registered with a different database than the first one tried. Here is
[how to scan a cat for a microchip](https://www.alleycat.org/resources/how-to-scan-a-cat-for-a-microchip/).
Then ask local groups.

Many apparent strays are owned. Do these checks before removing a cat from where it lives. A
collar suggests a home, but whose? As in [Stray, Pet, or Somewhere in Between?](/ideas/lost-and-found/), ownership
is a claim, not a fact. See also [When a Cat Goes Missing](/ideas/when-a-cat-goes-missing/) and
[Make Help Easier to Find Than Dumping](/ideas/help-easier-than-dumping/).

## No hard evidence yet

I haven't found studies showing paper collars work. The reports are anecdotal. It is cheap and
kind-hearted, and it might help, but I wouldn't promise more than that.

## Open questions

- What should the finder write on the strip: a phone number, a first name, or something else?
- Who prints them, and who pays?
- Is a tear-off sheet realistic, and at what cost?
- How do we keep it from being used on cats that shouldn't be handled?
- Would vets actually hand them out?

**The goal is simple: help the person who finds a cat and the person who lost one find each
other, without putting the cat at risk.**
